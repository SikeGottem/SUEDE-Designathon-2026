Unpacking the brief

Problem area

Appreciation can already exist without a socially obvious reason to express it.

On an ordinary day, saying it directly can feel unusually intense or out of place.

Challenge

A quick text is easy, but can feel too casual for the thought.

A letter or gift can feel meaningful, but too large or too difficult for the moment.

Working brief

Design an experience that reveals, communicates, questions, or redesigns an unwritten rule.

Supplied 4 September 2026. Official wording still to be verified.

Secondary
Research

Text is not the enemy.

Gratitude expressed by text can still be rewarding and can feel less emotionally risky than face-to-face expression.

The opportunity is not to replace chat. It is to give one thought a more distinct receiving context.

Sheldon & Yu, 2022
doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2021.1913639

But cues change how it lands.

Voice increased reported connection in controlled social exchanges, while email writers overestimated how clearly tone would be understood.

This supports a cue-and-ambiguity mechanism—not a claim that text is always worse.

Kumar & Epley, 2021 · Kruger et al., 2005
10.1037/xge0000962 · 10.1037/0022-3514.89.6.925

Secondary Research

Thoughtfulness is inferred.

Perceived benefactor effort predicted gratitude largely through perceived thoughtfulness.

Price did not reliably predict recipients' appreciation. More work is not automatically more care.

Design implication: make the sender's specific noticing visible, rather than adding friction for show.

Algoe, Haidt & Gable, 2008 · Flynn & Adams, 2009

Digital can still feel like yours.

Digital things can produce weaker psychological ownership when control, identity, and context are unclear.

Personal relevance, a legible place to return, and clear access or removal choices can strengthen that relationship.

Design implication: make one receiver-controlled object, not another feed post.

Atasoy & Morewedge, 2018 · Odom et al., 2012

These studies support design hypotheses. They do not validate Warm & Fuzzies.

RQ + Problem
Statement

Research Questions

  1. What makes ordinary-day appreciation feel natural, awkward, or excessive?
  2. What does a sender need to make their intention clear without creating reply pressure?
  3. Which qualities of text, voice, letters, and gifts should a digital object combine?

Problem Statement

People can feel appreciation but lack a comfortable script for expressing it on an ordinary day.

Existing channels split the moment between low-friction communication that can feel too casual and meaningful gestures that can feel like too much.

How might we make ordinary-day appreciation easier to send?
