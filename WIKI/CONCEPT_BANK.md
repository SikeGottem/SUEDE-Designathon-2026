# Cross-disciplinary concept bank

## Status and sources

Every concept on this page is an **exploratory hypothesis**. None is evidence, a recommendation, a selected direction or a settled decision. [Recording 15](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-15.md) and [Recording 16](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-16.md) seeded the sport/social-friction thread; [Recording 17](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-17.md), [Recording 19](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-19.md), and [Recording 20](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-20.md) seeded and refined the friendship-appreciation branch. Most concepts below are AI-assisted cross-disciplinary expansion, were not spoken by participants, and await human review. Candidate unwritten rules are separated in the evolving [Rule bank](RULE_BANK.md).

The cards deliberately vary the actor, moment and causal mechanism. A polished prototype cannot substitute for validating that the rule exists, matters in this context and is safe to intervene in.

## Expected competitor-default control group

Keep these as controls, not assumed winners:

- a crowdsourced wall or map of unwritten rules
- an etiquette chatbot or AI social coach
- a quiz that teaches newcomers how to behave
- an anonymous confession feed
- a voting app for rewriting community rules
- an AR overlay that labels norms in place
- an inclusion, bias or interruption dashboard
- a passive awareness poster, animation or campaign

These defaults usually treat the rule as information. The wider bank asks whether its real mechanism is permission, status, space, timing, labour, reciprocity, ambiguity, incentives or enforcement.

## Permission to enter

### 1. Open-Lane Flag

- **Rule:** “If you want to join the court, interrupt us and prove you belong.”
- **Actor / moment:** A solo newcomer watching an established pickup game between points.
- **Mechanism:** Current players raise a physical flag that creates a timed invitation window before the next game.
- **10-second demo:** The final whistle sounds; the flag rises and an empty player marker opens without the newcomer speaking.
- **Biggest risk:** Existing players may perform openness while informally excluding the entrant later.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Run three game turnovers with unfamiliar players and observe whether anyone enters without prompting or embarrassment.

### 2. Last-In Hosts Next

- **Rule:** “New people must find their own way into the group.”
- **Actor / moment:** The most recent member sees another newcomer approach a club gathering.
- **Mechanism:** Hosting duty passes to the last person admitted, creating a visible chain of sponsorship.
- **10-second demo:** A newcomer receives the host marker, then immediately crosses the threshold to welcome the next arrival.
- **Biggest risk:** Hosting becomes unwanted emotional labour for someone still unsettled.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Try one arrival cycle and ask both newcomers whether the handoff increased agency or pressure.

### 3. First-Mistake Veto

- **Rule:** “A beginner’s first mistake is evidence that they are not ready.”
- **Actor / moment:** A novice makes their first error in a hobby class or rehearsal.
- **Mechanism:** Only the novice may decide whether the group pauses, demonstrates, ignores or discusses the error.
- **10-second demo:** Several people begin correcting; the novice raises one hand and selects “keep going.”
- **Biggest risk:** The public choice may expose or overwhelm the novice.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Role-play one error with three response options and check whether the novice experiences control rather than scrutiny.

### 4. The Participating Chair

- **Rule:** “You may watch, but joining requires the confidence to ask.”
- **Actor / moment:** A spectator at a community workshop wants to participate midway.
- **Mechanism:** One clearly different chair circulates between observing and participating zones; sitting in it changes status without speech.
- **10-second demo:** A spectator moves the chair across the boundary and the activity opens around them.
- **Biggest risk:** The chair could publicly label uncertainty or become a novelty prop.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Place it in a five-minute activity and see whether observers understand and safely use it without explanation.

### 5. Rules Before Skill

- **Rule:** “If you do not already know the local version, you should stay out.”
- **Actor / moment:** A competent player encounters unfamiliar house rules just before play begins.
- **Mechanism:** Incumbents must physically enact one local rule and one permitted challenge before assessing anyone’s skill.
- **10-second demo:** The experienced newcomer catches the ball, but play pauses until an incumbent demonstrates the hidden boundary.
- **Biggest risk:** The preview could harden arbitrary rules rather than invite negotiation.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Ask one outsider to join after the enactment and test whether they can challenge a rule without social penalty.

### 6. Invitation Without Ownership

- **Rule:** “The person who invites you is responsible for where you go and who you meet.”
- **Actor / moment:** A guest enters a party where their only connection becomes unavailable.
- **Mechanism:** The invitation splits into three transferable introductions that the guest controls.
- **10-second demo:** The host leaves; the guest tears the invitation into three introductions and chooses where each goes.
- **Biggest risk:** Formal introductions may feel transactional or reveal social isolation.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Use the three-part invitation at a small gathering and see whether the guest initiates connections independently.

## Physical objects and material metaphors

### 7. Human Cache Expiry

- **Rule:** “Once we learn your preference, we can assume it forever.”
- **Actor / moment:** A friend is assigned an old role while a group plans an outing.
- **Mechanism:** Expectation cards visibly expire unless their owner refreshes them.
- **10-second demo:** A bell rings and “Ethan always books everything” fades from the card.
- **Biggest risk:** The device may trivialise identity changes or create constant renegotiation.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Expire five real group assumptions and ask whether any useful change occurs without confusion.

### 8. Burden-Breaking Heirloom

- **Rule:** “I endured this initiation, so the next person should too.”
- **Actor / moment:** A senior member hands an unpleasant tradition to a newcomer.
- **Mechanism:** The inherited object must be dismantled and rebuilt into support before it can be passed on.
- **10-second demo:** A heavy symbolic chain is cut and its links become handles for a shared tool.
- **Biggest risk:** Aestheticising hazing can minimise actual harm.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Use a harmless invented tradition and check whether participants understand transformation rather than destruction alone.

### 9. Responsibility Baton

- **Rule:** “If you notice a recurring problem, fixing it becomes your job.”
- **Actor / moment:** A junior worker notices the same shared-space failure again.
- **Mechanism:** A physical baton travels upward until it reaches someone with authority to remove the cause.
- **10-second demo:** A spill marker bypasses the habitual cleaner and lands in the manager’s hand.
- **Biggest risk:** Visible escalation could provoke retaliation or oversimplify shared responsibility.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Map one low-stakes workplace issue and see whether the baton reaches an actor with real leverage.

### 10. Rumour Weight

- **Rule:** “If enough people repeat it, it is safe to treat it as true.”
- **Actor / moment:** A student considers forwarding an unverified claim in a group chat.
- **Mechanism:** Each repetition adds a physical weight the repeater must carry until someone verifies or retracts it.
- **10-second demo:** A light sentence becomes too heavy to pass after three repetitions.
- **Biggest risk:** It may imply that truth is measurable by burden or discourage legitimate warnings.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Run three fictional claims through the object and test whether users pause without suppressing urgent speech.

### 11. Focus Veil

- **Rule:** “Closing yourself off in a shared space is rude.”
- **Actor / moment:** A person needing uninterrupted focus in a communal studio.
- **Mechanism:** A reversible tabletop veil communicates duration and offers a scheduled reopening point.
- **10-second demo:** The veil rises, reveals a return-time marker, then lowers around the work.
- **Biggest risk:** It may legitimise indefinite withdrawal or be inaccessible to people who cannot see it.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Use it for one work session and check whether interruptions drop without harming necessary coordination.

### 12. Promise with an Eraser

- **Rule:** “Changing your mind means your original commitment was dishonest.”
- **Actor / moment:** A volunteer realises they cannot complete a promised task.
- **Mechanism:** Commitments are written on an object designed to be revised while preserving a trace of what changed.
- **10-second demo:** A promise is erased, but its outline redirects into a smaller, honest commitment.
- **Biggest risk:** Easy revision could weaken accountability to others.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Let participants revise one commitment and ask affected people whether the new trace supports planning or feels evasive.

## Spaces and architecture

### 13. Meeting Without a Head

- **Rule:** “The person at the head of the table controls the discussion.”
- **Actor / moment:** A junior participant enters a critique with a senior decision-maker.
- **Mechanism:** A table with no stable front physically relocates the focal point after every contribution.
- **10-second demo:** The senior begins speaking; the surface rotates and the next focal point lands beside the junior.
- **Biggest risk:** Forced equality can conceal rather than change institutional power.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Compare one short discussion with fixed and shifting focal points; track attribution and interruption manually.

### 14. Shadow Commons

- **Rule:** “Shade belongs to whoever arrived first.”
- **Actor / moment:** A caregiver reaches a hot public courtyard after shaded seats are occupied.
- **Mechanism:** A movable canopy circulates through nomination by current occupants.
- **10-second demo:** One cord pull moves shade from a seated adult to a waiting pram.
- **Biggest risk:** Users may judge whose need is worthy or create conflict around nomination.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Use a scale model with conflicting scenarios and see whether participants can allocate shade without forced disclosure.

### 15. Quiet Gradient

- **Rule:** “A space is either social or silent, and everyone must fit the label.”
- **Actor / moment:** A student needs low conversation rather than absolute silence in a library.
- **Mechanism:** Acoustic and spatial thresholds create a continuous gradient whose boundaries occupants can adjust.
- **10-second demo:** Moving one soft partition turns a hard boundary into three distinct sound conditions.
- **Biggest risk:** The gradient may be illegible or fail people with sensory sensitivities.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Mock three zones with fabric and ask participants to place themselves without signs.

### 16. Empty-Centre Classroom

- **Rule:** “Knowledge comes from the person at the front.”
- **Actor / moment:** A student enters a classroom discussion where one authority normally anchors attention.
- **Mechanism:** All teaching material begins in an empty centre and must be moved outward by participants before anyone speaks.
- **10-second demo:** The lecturer reaches for the material, but it fragments toward the group.
- **Biggest risk:** Symbolic decentralisation may waste time without changing expertise or responsibility.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Run one ten-minute lesson and ask who participants perceived as able to contribute knowledge.

### 17. Reversible Territory

- **Rule:** “Leaving an object means this public place remains mine.”
- **Actor / moment:** A commuter encounters a bag occupying a scarce seat.
- **Mechanism:** Personal boundaries decay visibly when the owner is absent and can be renewed only through another person’s consent.
- **10-second demo:** An unattended border slowly retracts until the blocked seat re-enters the commons.
- **Biggest risk:** It may create conflict over belongings or ignore short necessary absences.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Test three absence lengths with a cardboard prototype and ask users when release feels fair.

### 18. The Door That Waits

- **Safety tag:** `role-play only` — do not automate or live-test a doorway before affected-person review and a fail-safe access plan.
- **Rule:** “The most confident body gets through a narrow entrance first.”
- **Actor / moment:** A mobility-aid user and a hurried pedestrian approach the same doorway.
- **Mechanism:** The door remains neutral until both occupy visible negotiation points, then opens to the path requiring more clearance.
- **10-second demo:** The hurried person arrives first, but the opening widens toward the mobility aid.
- **Biggest risk:** Automated judgement could be inaccurate, patronising or dangerous.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Role-play the geometry manually and test whether the negotiation is understandable without classifying bodies.

## Service roles and social rituals

### 19. No-Excuse Exit

- **Rule:** “Leaving a gathering requires a socially acceptable explanation.”
- **Actor / moment:** An overwhelmed guest wants to leave before the host expects.
- **Mechanism:** The host establishes a shared exit ritual that permits departure without apology or individual negotiation.
- **10-second demo:** A participant leaves mid-scene; everyone continues without asking why.
- **Biggest risk:** Departure may remain conspicuous or bypass genuine care and safety duties.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Use the ritual during one team break and ask whether leaving felt easier or more exposed.

### 20. Queue Proxy

- **Rule:** “Your body must remain in line continuously to deserve your place.”
- **Actor / moment:** A caregiver must briefly leave a clinic queue with a child.
- **Mechanism:** A rotating service steward temporarily embodies absent places without asking for private explanations.
- **10-second demo:** The caregiver steps away and the steward takes their floor mark.
- **Biggest risk:** The proxy role could be abused or create disputes about return time.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Simulate five queue interruptions and test whether strangers accept the transfer without disclosure.

### 21. Listener on Duty

- **Rule:** “The person who is hardest to understand must do all the repair work.”
- **Actor / moment:** A migrant encounters misunderstanding at a public service counter.
- **Mechanism:** A trained listener role rotates among staff and owns paraphrasing, confirmation and escalation.
- **10-second demo:** “Repeat that” is replaced by the staff member offering two interpretations for confirmation.
- **Biggest risk:** The role could become patronising or substitute for professional language support.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Role-play three misunderstandings and ask speakers whether effort and dignity actually shifted.

### 22. Repair Has Stages

- **Rule:** “Once someone apologises, the harmed person should restore social comfort.”
- **Actor / moment:** A teammate receives an apology immediately after a hurtful incident.
- **Mechanism:** A facilitated sequence separates acknowledgment, safety, requested repair, time and optional reconciliation.
- **10-second demo:** The apology ends, but the repair object remains visibly incomplete.
- **Biggest risk:** Formal process can coerce disclosure or place more labour on the harmed person.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Role-play a fictional low-stakes conflict and stop if the harmed role feels pressured to forgive.

### 23. Closure Steward

- **Rule:** “The event is over when the audience leaves; the remaining work belongs to whoever stays.”
- **Actor / moment:** A volunteer reaches the end of a community event.
- **Mechanism:** A visible steward closes the experience only after distributing exit, cleanup and recovery roles.
- **10-second demo:** The applause triggers a second opening gesture that reveals the closing crew.
- **Biggest risk:** Guests may feel conscripted or volunteers may still absorb coordination.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Add the ritual to one small event and compare who remains for cleanup.

### 24. Bystander Rehearsal

- **Rule:** “If the harmed person says nothing, everyone else should stay neutral.”
- **Actor / moment:** A witness notices a subtle exclusion during a social interaction.
- **Mechanism:** A facilitator rehearses several low-escalation interventions before the real event begins.
- **10-second demo:** The same exclusion repeats three times, each interrupted by a different bystander move.
- **Biggest risk:** Scripted confidence may lead to unsafe intervention or speaking over the affected person.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Test three fictional scenes and ask the affected role which intervention, if any, preserved agency.

## Performance and choreography

### 25. Secret Rules Theatre

- **Safety tag:** `role-play only` — informed consent and debrief are mandatory; never conceal participation.
- **Rule:** “Insiders may know how belonging works; newcomers learn by failing.”
- **Actor / moment:** A newcomer enters an unfamiliar social scene.
- **Mechanism:** Half the participants receive a secret behavioural rule; roles reverse after one breach.
- **10-second demo:** A judge is corrected for breaking a rule they were never given.
- **Biggest risk:** Manufactured humiliation may become the spectacle.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Run a consented five-minute scene and ask whether participants recall the asymmetry rather than only the trick.

### 26. Hold the Rest

- **Rule:** “Someone must fill silence before it becomes awkward.”
- **Actor / moment:** A person processes a difficult question during a group conversation.
- **Mechanism:** Musical-rest logic turns silence into a collectively sustained gesture.
- **10-second demo:** Everyone holds one visible pose until the thinker releases the pause.
- **Biggest risk:** The ritual could intensify attention on the person needing time.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Compare one ordinary pause with one held pause and ask whether thinking felt protected or spotlighted.

### 27. Consent Tempo

- **Rule:** “Once a dance begins, slowing or stopping means rejecting your partner.”
- **Actor / moment:** Someone becomes uncomfortable during a social dance.
- **Mechanism:** Contact improvisation makes pace and distance continuously renegotiable.
- **10-second demo:** One dancer freezes; the other steps back and reopens space without drama.
- **Biggest risk:** Movement cues may be missed or falsely interpreted as consent.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Teach two cues, run a short duet and verify both dancers can stop safely without explanation.

### 28. Status Costume Exchange

- **Rule:** “Authority should look composed, expensive and certain.”
- **Actor / moment:** A junior creative presents uncertain early work to senior reviewers.
- **Mechanism:** Costume and role exchange temporarily detach confidence signals from decision power.
- **10-second demo:** The senior removes the authority garment and hands it to the person presenting doubt.
- **Biggest risk:** Symbolic role-play may trivialise entrenched class or workplace hierarchy.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Run one critique with exchanged visual roles and ask whether uncertainty becomes safer to express.

### 29. Idea Echo with Lineage

- **Rule:** “An idea belongs to whoever says it most confidently.”
- **Actor / moment:** A quiet contributor hears their suggestion repeated and credited elsewhere.
- **Mechanism:** Choral repetition must include the original speaker’s name and transformation path.
- **10-second demo:** A copied phrase returns through the room carrying a visible chain of attribution.
- **Biggest risk:** Tracking every contribution could make conversation rigid or expose ownership disputes.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Use it for one brainstorming round and test whether attribution improves without reducing combination.

### 30. The Audience Performs the Standard

- **Rule:** “The presenter alone must prove they belong here.”
- **Actor / moment:** A nervous participant begins a public pitch or audition.
- **Mechanism:** The audience must first perform the behaviour it expects from the presenter.
- **10-second demo:** Before judging confidence, the panel collectively delivers the opening line under the same constraint.
- **Biggest risk:** It could antagonise evaluators or become theatrical deflection.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Use one harmless performance constraint and ask whether evaluation criteria become clearer or merely awkward.

## Incentives, policy and governance

### 31. Sunset Ceremony

- **Rule:** “Traditions continue unless someone is brave enough to object.”
- **Actor / moment:** A young adult faces an unwanted family or community tradition during planning.
- **Mechanism:** Every tradition expires and requires a reason plus affected-person consent to renew.
- **10-second demo:** A familiar ritual card fades until somebody argues for it.
- **Biggest risk:** Explicit renewal could trigger family conflict or erase protective tradition.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Use fictional traditions and test whether participants distinguish consent from majority preference.

### 32. Shared Error Budget

- **Rule:** “One mistake proves that person is unreliable.”
- **Actor / moment:** A newcomer makes an error during their first collaborative task.
- **Mechanism:** Mistakes draw from a group-owned budget replenished through repair.
- **10-second demo:** The error token moves away from the person and into the team’s process vessel.
- **Biggest risk:** Shared responsibility may erase individual accountability for harmful choices.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Classify three errors and see whether participants agree which are systemic, individual or mixed.

### 33. Honest RSVP Market

- **Rule:** “Declining is rude, so say yes or maybe even when you cannot attend.”
- **Actor / moment:** Someone receives several invitations in the same week.
- **Mechanism:** A finite set of time pieces makes capacity visible without requiring excuses.
- **10-second demo:** Three polite maybes collapse into one honest commitment when only one piece remains.
- **Biggest risk:** Quantifying relationships may feel transactional or expose priorities.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Use fictional invitations and ask whether people become more truthful without feeling socially punished.

### 34. Dissent as Precedent

- **Rule:** “We do it this way because we always have.”
- **Actor / moment:** A new committee member encounters a repeated organisational practice.
- **Mechanism:** Every renewed practice preserves one dissent and a condition for reopening it.
- **10-second demo:** A “tradition” stamp reveals a folded objection underneath.
- **Biggest risk:** Recording dissent without power to act could become decorative participation.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Apply it to one low-stakes procedure and check whether the reopening condition ever changes discussion.

### 35. Decision Power Pays

- **Rule:** “The least powerful person absorbs the inconvenience created by the decision.”
- **Actor / moment:** A casual worker receives a last-minute roster change.
- **Mechanism:** Every change incurs a burden piece held by the person with authority until compensation or consent resolves it.
- **10-second demo:** Moving one shift places a visible weight on the scheduler’s side of the table.
- **Biggest risk:** A symbolic cost may normalise harm instead of preventing it.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Map three scheduling changes and test whether decision-makers choose differently when they must resolve the burden.

### 36. Jury of Absences

- **Rule:** “If you are not in the room, your interests do not count.”
- **Actor / moment:** A resident cannot attend a neighbourhood decision meeting.
- **Mechanism:** Attendees draw evidence-based absent-stakeholder briefs before voting.
- **10-second demo:** A unanimous vote reverses when one sealed absence is opened.
- **Biggest risk:** Present people may invent or stereotype absent perspectives.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Compare represented claims with one real absent stakeholder’s response.

## Accessibility and interdependence

### 37. Crip-Time Contract

- **Safety tag:** `co-design required` — no disability simulation or live deployment before affected-person review.
- **Rule:** “If you cannot work at the standard pace, you are less committed.”
- **Actor / moment:** A chronically ill student begins a timed collaborative task.
- **Mechanism:** The group negotiates outputs through variable energy and recovery pieces rather than identical hours.
- **10-second demo:** One rigid clock separates into several valid paths to the same outcome.
- **Biggest risk:** Participants may be pressured to publicly justify fluctuating capacity.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Run one task using private capacity pieces and ask whether planning improves without disclosure.

### 38. Many-Way Instructions

- **Safety tag:** `co-design required` — no disability simulation or live deployment before affected-person review.
- **Rule:** “The normal format is for everyone; alternatives are special accommodations.”
- **Actor / moment:** A participant receives instructions at a public workshop.
- **Mechanism:** No single format is complete; tactile, visual, spoken and demonstrated fragments must be combined.
- **10-second demo:** The printed instruction stops halfway and only a physical gesture completes it.
- **Biggest risk:** Deliberate incompleteness may create avoidable barriers or confusion.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Test the activity with varied access needs and verify every person has at least one independent route.

### 39. Interdependence Relay

- **Safety tag:** `co-design required` — no disability simulation or live deployment before affected-person review.
- **Rule:** “Competent people finish without asking for help.”
- **Actor / moment:** A participant with uneven access enters a team challenge.
- **Mechanism:** Tools and capabilities are distributed so no individual can complete the task alone.
- **10-second demo:** The apparent strongest participant reaches a dead end until another person supplies access.
- **Biggest risk:** Simulated constraints can caricature disability.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Test the dependency structure without imitating impairments and ask whether requesting help feels skilled rather than deficient.

### 40. Needs Without Names

- **Safety tag:** `co-design required` — no disability simulation or live deployment before affected-person review.
- **Rule:** “Only people with a diagnosis may change the environment.”
- **Actor / moment:** Someone needs lower stimulation before entering a shared activity.
- **Mechanism:** Everyone selects environmental needs anonymously; no reason or identity is attached.
- **10-second demo:** Light and sound change because several unlabeled need markers align.
- **Biggest risk:** Majority aggregation can erase one person’s essential requirement.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Introduce one high-impact minority need and verify the negotiation protects it without forced disclosure.

### 41. Fatigue Handoff

- **Safety tag:** `co-design required` — no disability simulation or live deployment before affected-person review.
- **Rule:** “Stopping halfway means you failed the group.”
- **Actor / moment:** A person’s energy drops during a collaborative physical task.
- **Mechanism:** Work is designed with named handoff points where continuation, pause and role change are equally legitimate.
- **10-second demo:** A participant places their piece down; another continues from the marked seam with no restart.
- **Biggest risk:** Others may still resent the person or permanently assign them lesser roles.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Run a task with two planned handoffs and ask whether stopping changes perceived competence.

### 42. Caption Begins the Event

- **Safety tag:** `co-design required` — no disability simulation or live deployment before affected-person review.
- **Rule:** “Access can be added after the main communication is finished.”
- **Actor / moment:** A Deaf or hard-of-hearing attendee enters a live presentation.
- **Mechanism:** The event cannot begin until its non-audio communication channel performs the opening.
- **10-second demo:** The room stays dark until the caption or visual-language layer delivers the first line.
- **Biggest risk:** A symbolic opening may hide poor access during the rest of the experience.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Audit the complete five-minute version with a Deaf participant, not only the opening gesture.

## Care, labour and invisible maintenance

### 43. The Care Table

- **Rule:** “Meals and gatherings appear through one person’s effortless care.”
- **Actor / moment:** The default organiser prepares a shared household meal.
- **Mechanism:** Ordinary objects unfold into the prerequisite tasks behind them and require redistribution.
- **10-second demo:** One plate expands into shopping, remembering, timing, serving and cleanup.
- **Biggest risk:** The reveal may shame families or reproduce gender stereotypes.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Map one real meal with two households and see whether overlooked work becomes recognisable.

### 44. Closing Shift First

- **Rule:** “Cleanup happens after the real work.”
- **Actor / moment:** Volunteers prepare to run a workshop or community event.
- **Mechanism:** Teardown is assigned and physically rehearsed before the opening activity.
- **10-second demo:** Starting the event simultaneously unfurls its closing sequence.
- **Biggest risk:** Participants may feel conscripted before receiving value.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Rehearse one small event and compare cleanup distribution with the usual approach.

### 45. Maintenance Credits

- **Rule:** “Only visible creators deserve public credit.”
- **Actor / moment:** Setup and operations workers prepare a showcase.
- **Mechanism:** Audience entry requires performing one named maintenance action from a live credit sequence.
- **10-second demo:** A ticket becomes “coil a cable for Mina, lighting.”
- **Biggest risk:** Brief participation may romanticise sustained low-paid labour.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Ask actual maintenance workers whether the action increases recognition or feels appropriative.

### 46. Compliment Auction

- **Rule:** “Praise belongs to whoever presents the finished output.”

- **Mechanism:** A finite, hand-built digital or physical envelope is sealed and delivered after a chosen delay, making waiting and craft part of the communication rather than adding a feed.
- **10-second demo:** A rough collage folds shut; the instant-send control disappears and a delivery moment is chosen.
- **Biggest risk:** Decorative friction may imitate sincerity without making expression safer or more specific.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Compare the same sentiment as an instant template and a five-minute delayed artefact; ask sender and recipient what changed besides appearance.

### 98. No-Reply Seal

- **Rule:** “Receiving a sincere message creates an immediate obligation to match it.”
- **Actor / moment:** A recipient opens an appreciative note but does not know how to respond.
- **Mechanism:** The sender chooses a visible no-reply covenant that closes the interaction without signalling rejection; the recipient may reopen it later but owes nothing now.
- **10-second demo:** The note opens, then the reply field resolves into “received—nothing due.”
- **Biggest risk:** A UI label cannot erase reciprocity pressure if the relationship still enforces it.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Role-play receipt with and without the seal; measure perceived obligation, freedom, and warmth rather than clicks.

### 99. Quiet Friendship Beacon

- **Rule:** “If friends are not in regular contact, one of them must explain the distance before the bond can feel active again.”
- **Actor / moment:** Someone thinks of a long-standing friend they have not seen in months.
- **Mechanism:** A single-use, content-light signal communicates continuing regard without reopening a conversation, explaining absence, or requesting a reply.
- **10-second demo:** One small light arrives with “still here; no catch-up debt” and then expires.
- **Biggest risk:** A low-effort ping may become another empty streak mechanic or substitute for needed repair.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Ask low-contact friends whether the signal legitimises their bond or merely reminds them of neglected contact.

### 100. Shared Memory Passbook

- **Rule:** “The more expressive friend should maintain the shared history; everyone else can simply enjoy it later.”
- **Actor / moment:** Two friends remember the same event differently while apart.
- **Mechanism:** A deliberately finite passbook moves between them; each page requires one memory and one present-day meaning before it can move on.
- **10-second demo:** One photo receives two annotations, then the book locks that spread and passes hands.
- **Biggest risk:** Structured memory work may become homework or expose unequal emotional labour.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Give one blank spread to three friendship pairs and observe whether contribution becomes genuinely shared.

### 101. Appreciation Prediction Booth

- **Rule:** “Saying what I value will make the other person more awkward than happy.”
- **Actor / moment:** A pair of consenting friends independently anticipates and receives a specific appreciation.
- **Mechanism:** Sender predictions and recipient experience are privately recorded, then the gap is revealed only to the pair as a lived experiment.
- **10-second demo:** “They will feel 8/10 awkward” flips beside the recipient's private “2/10 awkward, 9/10 valued.”
- **Biggest risk:** It could manufacture intimacy, expose a mismatch, or turn care into a score.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Run a paper role-play with consenting pairs and stop if either person does not want the comparison revealed.

### 102. Friendship Maintenance Constitution

- **Rule:** “Real friends should already know how often to reply, meet, joke, check in, and repair silence.”
- **Actor / moment:** Friends with different contact rhythms interpret the same gap differently.
- **Mechanism:** A temporary, revisable pact lets each person define what closeness does and does not require; disagreements become negotiable rules rather than private tests.
- **10-second demo:** “Reply within a day” is crossed out and replaced with “urgent needs are named; silence is not punishment.”
- **Biggest risk:** Formalising friendship can feel managerial, coercive, or remove welcome ambiguity.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Let pairs negotiate only one response-time assumption and ask whether clarity increases comfort or kills spontaneity.

### 103. Ordinary-Day Signal

- **Rule:** “An unexpected sincere note must mean a crisis, confession, birthday, or goodbye; ordinary care needs an excuse.”
- **Actor / moment:** A friend receives a specific appreciation without a recognised occasion.
- **Mechanism:** A sender-authored context cue marks the note as ordinary-day care without scripting its content, triggering a prompt, or demanding reassurance.
- **10-second demo:** The note opens with “nothing happened; I chose today,” then the cue disappears and leaves the sender's own words.
- **Biggest risk:** The reassurance becomes another template, feels alarmingly defensive, or normalises the very rule it questions.
- **Smallest falsification test:** Give willing recipients the same sincere note bare versus with the ordinary-day cue; compare perceived alarm, authorship, sincerity, and pressure.

## No-convergence checklist

Before combining, promoting or selecting anything:

- [ ] The official brief wording has been verified.
- [ ] The unwritten rule is stated in lived language, not as a broad topic.
- [ ] A real actor and repeated moment have replaced the imagined generic user.
- [ ] Evidence supports the rule’s existence, enforcement and consequence.
- [ ] The beneficiary, enforcer, burdened person, outsider and exception are identified.
- [ ] At least four surviving concepts use different causal mechanisms, not different interfaces.
- [ ] The obvious app or information product remains only a control.
- [ ] At least one concept changes the enforcer, incentive, space, ritual or institutional burden.
- [ ] At least one concept removes rather than adds an interaction.
- [ ] Revealing the rule cannot quietly strengthen, normalise or weaponise it.
- [ ] The affected person is not required to disclose, educate, perform pain or absorb more work.
- [ ] A non-digital mechanism has been tested before claiming technology is necessary.
- [ ] The ten-second demo shows the mechanism and outcome, not merely attractive presentation.
- [ ] The smallest test could genuinely falsify the concept.
- [ ] No concept is described as validated, recommended or selected without the required idea and judging gates.
- [ ] The team can explain what human observation, judgment and revision changed after AI-assisted divergence.
