## Appendix slide copy

### Why form can change meaning

**1. Relational cues**

Voice can create more connection than text in controlled social exchanges, while email experiments show that writers often overestimate how clearly tone will be understood. This supports a cue-and-ambiguity mechanism—not a hierarchy in which text is always worse.

*Boundary: these studies were not tests of an asynchronous appreciation artifact.* [Kumar & Epley, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000962); [Kruger et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.89.6.925)

**2. Legible thoughtfulness**

In natural gift exchanges, recipients’ perceived benefactor effort predicted gratitude, largely through perceived thoughtfulness. But effort is only a cue: price did not predict recipients’ appreciation, and later replication work found mixed effects of effort information on object valuation.

*Design implication: surface the sender’s specific reason or memory; do not celebrate time spent or add friction for show.* [Algoe, Haidt & Gable, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/1528-3542.8.3.425); [Flynn & Adams, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2008.11.003); [Ziano et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.87489)

**3. Psychological ownership**

Across five experiments, equivalent digital goods were generally valued less than physical goods, partly because they created weaker psychological ownership. Expected ownership, identity relevance, and perceived control bounded the gap.

*Design implication: give the receiver a legible personal place plus clear keep, reopen, remove, and access choices; do not promise permanence or parity with physical keepsakes.* [Atasoy & Morewedge, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucx102); [Odom et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2207789)

**Bottom line:** Warm & Fuzzies combines three testable cues—human presence, visible authorship, and receiver control. Published evidence makes those cues plausible. Only a matched receiver test can show whether this particular combination feels more meaningful than a normal message.

## 1. Text, voice, and relational cues

### What is supported

Kumar and Epley tested communication medium in two experiments. In a field experiment, people reconnecting with an old friend reported a stronger bond by phone than by email. A controlled synchronous study then compared text, audio, and video while holding the interaction task more constant; voice-based interaction produced greater connection, enjoyment, and liking than text without increasing awkwardness. Video did not reliably exceed audio. The result supports the value of vocal cues, not a claim that every voice note beats every text. [Kumar & Epley, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32915017/)

Kruger and colleagues ran five experiments on tone in email. In the direct voice-versus-email comparison, partners identified intended serious or sarcastic tone more accurately from recorded voice than from identical emailed statements, while senders in both conditions expected high accuracy. The mechanism was sender egocentrism: knowing one’s own intended tone makes the written message feel clearer than it is to a reader. The task concerned sarcasm and seriousness, not appreciation, so the result supports ambiguity risk rather than general misunderstanding. [Kruger et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16393025/)

In a within-subject study of 58 young women communicating with close friends, all four conditions—in person, video, audio, and instant messaging—produced connection. Bonding was highest in person and lower in text, but participants also adapted textual communication with affiliation cues such as laughter, capitals, and emoticons. The small, narrow sample and synchronous conversation limit generalisation. [Sherman et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.5817/CP2013-2-3)

### The necessary counterclaim

Three studies in US and Taiwanese samples compared imagined, recalled, and enacted gratitude expression. They found that texting could still be rewarding and appeared less emotionally risky than face-to-face expression; in the enacted longitudinal study, gratitude conditions improved expresser wellbeing and differed little by communication method. The outcomes were mainly the expresser’s wellbeing, not recipient-rated meaning, so the study does not prove equivalence for the receiver. It does rule out “a text does not count.” [Sheldon & Yu, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2021.1913639)

### Pitch-safe conclusion

> Text is a valid gratitude channel. The opportunity is not to repair a broken medium; it is to test whether additional relational cues and a distinct receiving context help a particular appreciation feel more clearly authored and held.

## 2. Effort, thoughtfulness, and gratitude

### Strongest recipient evidence

Algoe, Haidt, and Gable studied natural “Big Sister Week” gift and benefit exchanges across three US sororities. Seventy-eight recipients completed 496 near-event records. Perceived effort predicted gratitude, and perceived thoughtfulness explained much of that association: effort predicted thoughtfulness strongly, while the direct effort–gratitude relationship became much smaller after thoughtfulness entered the model. This is real relationship behaviour but a narrow, predominantly young White female sorority context, and it is correlational rather than a clean randomised message test. [Algoe, Haidt & Gable, 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2692821/)

Zhang and Epley’s four experiments add an important design condition: receivers do not always infer care automatically. Gift quality and fit often dominate unless the giver’s thought is made salient or the receiver is prompted to consider it. That makes a specific memory or reason more useful than a generic “this took effort” signal. [Zhang & Epley, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029223)

### Guardrails against “more work = more love”

The original effort-heuristic experiments found that people rated a poem, painting, and suit of armour more favourably when told they required more time or effort. A later preregistered replication programme found mixed results: a revised replication supported a smaller effect on liking and quality but not monetary value, while another attempt failed. This is indirect object-evaluation evidence, not a relational-message law. [Kruger et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-1031(03)00065-9); [Ziano et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.87489)

Gift-price studies found an asymmetry: givers expected more expensive gifts to produce more appreciation, but recipients reported no corresponding price–appreciation relationship. Other experiments found that recipients often preferred gifts they had explicitly requested over unrequested choices that givers expected to seem thoughtful. Effort must therefore serve fit and care; it cannot compensate for weak substance. [Flynn & Adams, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2008.11.003); [Gino & Flynn, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2011.03.015)

### Pitch-safe conclusion

> Perceived effort can become gratitude when it helps the receiver infer thoughtful, responsive care. Warm & Fuzzies should make the sender’s specific noticing legible—not make the process slow, expensive, or performative.

## 3. Digital psychological ownership

### What is supported

Atasoy and Morewedge ran five experiments across souvenir photos, books, and films. Participants generally paid more, were willing to pay more, or were more willing to purchase physical versions than equivalent digital goods. Stronger psychological ownership of physical goods partly explained the difference. Expected ownership, identity relevance, and perceived control bounded the physical–digital gap. The experiments concern consumer value, not intimate mementos, but the manipulated mechanisms are directly useful design inputs. [Atasoy & Morewedge, 2018](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/44/6/1343/4430295)

Odom and colleagues’ in-home fieldwork with 13 UK participants found that digital materials could be identity-relevant, yet third-party platform dependence, uncertain location, copies, access, and deletion complicated the feeling of possession. Their design implications favour a circumscribed personal place, preserved context, and legible access and deletion rights. The sample is qualitative and small, so use it as design guidance rather than prevalence evidence. [Odom et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2207789)

A 14-month Photobox deployment across three households found that selectively resurfacing personal digital photos could support anticipation, reflection, renewed appreciation, and revisitation. Its sample is far too small for a population claim, but it reinforces that meaning comes through return and context, not merely infinite storage. [Odom et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557178)

### Pitch-safe conclusion

> Digital does not automatically feel owned. Receiver control, personal relevance, provenance, and a legible place to return can support psychological ownership. Warm & Fuzzies implements those as design hypotheses, not proven effects.

## Recommended primary test before presenting a causal claim

Use one short matched-content receiver test. The sender creates one genuine ordinary-day appreciation. Present the same core content in:

1. a normal text-message frame;
2. a simple voice-note frame; and
3. the Warm & Fuzzies artifact.

Randomise condition order where possible. Ask receivers privately:

- “How warm did this feel?”
- “How clearly could you tell why they chose you?”
- “How thoughtful did this feel?”
- “How pressured did you feel to reply?”
- “Would you want to keep or revisit this?”
- “Which version felt most like something that was yours?”
- “What specifically made it feel that way?”

Record the qualitative reason before showing the hypothesis. Do not claim causation from a tiny convenience sample; show counts and short quotations as directional prototype evidence.

## Judge-lens review

**Verdict: Proceed with conditions.**

- **Problem identification:** Stronger if the deck says “some appreciation loses cues or distinctness in everyday chat,” not “text is meaningless.”
- **Solution approach:** Stronger when every feature maps to a mechanism: authored reason → thoughtfulness; voice/marks → relational cues; cabinet and receiver controls → psychological ownership and revisitation.
- **Design innovation:** The combination is plausible, but the individual mechanisms are established patterns. Innovation must be demonstrated in the receiving experience, not asserted from citations.
- **Visual communication:** One spoken bridge plus one appendix slide preserves story pace. A new live-deck literature slide would interrupt the pitch.
- **Presentation skills:** The counterclaim that texting can work increases credibility and pre-empts an obvious judge objection.

**Weakest judging angle:** Evidence quality. The mechanisms are supported by adjacent research, but no study directly tests this artifact against an ordinary message.

**Main trade-off:** The nuanced claim is more credible but less immediately dramatic than “text is meaningless.”

**Next evidence:** One matched-content receiver comparison.

## Limitations and stopping rule

- Most evidence comes from US participants; the ownership fieldwork is UK-based. None establishes an Australian prevalence rate.
- Studies use different outcomes: connection, tone accuracy, expresser wellbeing, gratitude, consumer valuation, and digital-possession experience. They cannot be collapsed into one “meaningfulness” metric.
- Gift, service, synchronous conversation, and digital-goods studies are adjacent evidence, not direct tests of the current artifact.
- No located study directly compares ordinary text, voice note, physical letter, and a bounded multimedia appreciation object with identical content.
- The research stopped after each transcript question had direct supporting evidence, a meaningful contradiction or boundary, and a concrete test implication. Additional adjacent literature would add volume faster than decision value.
