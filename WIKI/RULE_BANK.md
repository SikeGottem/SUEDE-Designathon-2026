# Rule bank

## Status and source boundary

This is a rapid-ideation bank, not a research finding, decision record, or shortlist. Every rule below is a **hypothesis/prompt** to investigate with people before it supports a concept claim. It deliberately moves beyond the team’s initial sport/social-platform thread.

The sport/social-friction thread was seeded by [Recording 15](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-15.md) and [Recording 16](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-16.md); [Recording 17](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-17.md), [Recording 19](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-19.md), and [Recording 20](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-20.md) added friendship-appreciation and online-interaction hypotheses. The wider set below is AI-assisted cross-disciplinary expansion and was not spoken by the participants unless the raw transcript shows it. It awaits human review; none of it is selected.

An indented **Designable threshold** means only that a specific test moment has been written. It is not a quality marker or shortlist.

**Affected-person co-design is required** before advancing rules involving disability, queer disclosure, personal safety, poverty, grief or high-stakes conflict. Do not use simulations that ask participants to imitate a marginalised identity.

## Rule grammar

`Around here, [person] is expected to [behaviour], even though nobody says so — enforced by [signal/consequence].`

This form prevents topic-only ideation. It forces:

- a specific person who is carrying the norm;
- an observable behaviour at a repeatable moment;
- the social, spatial, institutional, or economic enforcement mechanism; and
- a consequence worth testing rather than an abstract annoyance.

### How to mine a rule

1. Ask someone for the exact moment they hesitate, hide, over-prepare, apologise, or choose not to act.
2. Write their lived language into the grammar above; do not start with an app, feature, or demographic label.
3. Identify who has to act first, who gets to stay passive, and what signal makes the rule feel real.
4. Invert one element: make the insider explain, make the institution carry the work, make the boundary visible, or create a dignified refusal.
5. Test the smallest threshold action before designing a polished solution.

## Cross-domain patterns to pull on

- **The newcomer tax:** the least-informed person must initiate, ask, disclose, or make themselves smaller before entry becomes possible.
- **Invisible coordination work:** one person remembers, notices, translates, repairs, or plans because everyone else can rely on their silence.
- **Legibility versus dignity:** people need a way to communicate need, availability, boundary, or capacity without having to disclose more than is safe.
- **Default neutrality that is not neutral:** a seat, entrance, message setting, bill split, or participation style looks ordinary but advantages the person already fluent in it.
- **The emotional cost of refusal:** people avoid clear invitations, limits, and repairs because there is no socially dignified way to say no.
- **Backstage labour made invisible:** the apparent ease of a family, workplace, city, or online identity depends on effort somebody is expected not to name.
- **Informal authority:** the real decision-maker, standard, or access route differs from the formal one.
- **Time as enforcement:** the rule becomes strongest during a three-second threshold, a late arrival, a deadline, or the period after a rupture.

## Anti-obvious checks before expanding any rule

- If the response is “make an app that connects people,” pause. What exact threshold action must change?
- If the rule describes everyone, narrow it to one person, place, and repeated moment.
- If the consequence is only awkwardness, ask what opportunity, safety, dignity, money, belonging, or voice is actually lost.
- If the intervention asks the burdened person to disclose more, explain why the burden cannot shift to an insider, host, institution, space, or default.
- If a sign, policy, service, spatial change, ritual, or prop could test the mechanism faster than a screen, start there.
- If the idea only explains a rule, require participants to experience either the current enforcement or a changed condition.
- If the mechanism needs universal adoption, find a version that works for one participant at one real moment.
- If the rule could be formal policy rather than social expectation, identify the informal signals that keep it alive.
- If the concept risks humiliation, coercion, exposure, or forced interaction, build consent, exit, and recovery into the first rough test.
- If the pitch could fit another problem after swapping the labels, return to the specific enforcement signal.

## Twelve fast interview / observation questions

1. Tell me about the last time you wanted to do something but hesitated because you were not sure what was socially allowed.
2. What did you notice that told you “this is not for me” or “I should not ask”?
3. Who could have made that moment easier without you having to explain yourself?
4. What would have felt like a dignified way to enter, ask, decline, or leave?
5. When did you first learn that this was “how things are done”?
6. What happens to someone who ignores this rule, even a little?
7. Who benefits when everyone follows it? Who does more work because of it?
8. Is there a particular object, layout, phrase, notification, or body-language cue that enforces it?
9. What do people currently do to work around it, and what does that workaround cost?
10. What would make an attempt to change the rule feel unsafe, embarrassing, or patronising?
11. Can you show me the exact place, screen, or sequence where the decision happens?
12. If one condition changed for thirty seconds, what would let you act differently?
