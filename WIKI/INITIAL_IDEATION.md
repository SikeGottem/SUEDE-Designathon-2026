## One transcript-derived hypothesis

The team has identified a potentially useful tension, but not yet a designable problem: people may want a social outcome while an unwritten rule makes initiating it feel intrusive, unsafe, status-risky, or embarrassing. A generic matching app is already an established control. The next comparison must test whether the real mechanism is permission, status, safety, resource access, institutional burden, incentives, spatial design, or something that should not create more interaction at all.

The immediate job is to find a rule whose burden, enforcer, exception, and consequence can be observed. Do not treat “social anxiety,” “phones,” or “people do not talk to strangers” as sufficient problem statements.

## Competitor answer: generic matching is an existing control

The team correctly anticipated that fill-in sport may exist. It does. Existing products are strongest at **coordination**: location, time, number, payment, RSVP, skill labels, teams, and no-shows. They do not prove that an unfamiliar group in a public court will truthfully signal welcome, that a solo newcomer will trust them, or that joining changes the group’s experience positively.

If sport remains in the set, it must answer one of these narrower questions:

- How can a group make a genuine invitation visible without stopping its game or feeling exposed?
- How can a newcomer know the social and skill expectations before crossing the court boundary?
- How is “not today” communicated without rejection or humiliation?
- What allows a newcomer to become a known regular rather than a one-off fill-in?
- What does a physical court, host, or recurring ritual do better than another sports platform?

## Specific risk notes

### Phone claim

Treat phone use as a contextual signal, not a villain. A 2005 observational study found mobile phones both conformed to and transformed public-interaction norms rather than replacing social life in a simple way ([Humphreys](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444805058164)). The correct research question is: *when do headphones, posture, eye contact, group configuration, or a phone actually mean “do not interrupt” here?*

### Priority seating and hidden disability

This territory has a rich unwritten-rule conflict, but it cannot be designed from assumptions. TfNSW’s own material acknowledges hidden needs and nervousness about asking; hidden-disability Sunflower identifiers are designed to signal a need to **staff**, not to force public disclosure ([TfNSW access guidance](https://transportnsw.info/travel-info/using-public-transport/accessible-travel/access-to-trains-in-nsw)). A credible next move would require conversations with disabled travellers and advocates before any concept claims to solve it. Reject surveillance, forced proof, seat-ranking, public shaming, or an intervention that moves disclosure burden onto the person needing support.

### Commuting and belonging

The observed need is not “make a four-hour commute more productive.” The likely rule worth testing is: *commuters are expected to privately absorb time, cost, fatigue, and missed belonging, then participate as though they arrived with the same spare capacity as resident students.* A 2025 qualitative study describes time/cost constraining informal peer support and social activity ([study](https://doi.org/10.1080/13596748.2025.2522547)); it does not prove that every commuter wants a social intervention.

### Social-norm interventions

Make the desired action safe, voluntary, specific, and visibly approved. Do not announce the prevalence of withdrawal, stigma, or non-compliance. Social interventions need an opt-in, a graceful decline, and a way not to expose someone’s identity or need.

## Problem-first × space-first method

Use two columns on the same wall, then only develop intersections that have a real causal chain.

| Rule / tension lane | Context lane | The necessary intersection test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Do not impose on strangers.” | pickup court, train platform, studio, O-Week | Who wants what; who gives permission; what makes initiation risky? |
| “Do not reveal a need.” | priority seating, accessible queue, classroom | Who bears the request/disclosure burden; what happens if they do nothing? |
| “You should already know.” | first class, college, critique, club | How is the rule learned; who benefits from insider knowledge? |
| “Do not waste others’ time.” | commute, office hours, group work | Is the rule protecting a person, or hiding an institutional inequity? |
| “Act like you belong before you belong.” | sport, campus, creative scene | What visible ritual keeps newcomers peripheral? |
| “If I say how much you matter, I will make this friendship weird.” | birthday, move-away, ordinary week, group hang | Is the sender avoiding awkwardness, obligation, intensity, or a specific response expectation? |
| “If I care, I should reply quickly and stay visible.” | group chat, long-distance friendship, work friendship | Does contact frequency actually operate as proof of friendship for the people involved, and who wants an alternative? |

**Done condition for a territory:** the team can complete the sentence: “For [specific person] in [specific moment], [unwritten rule] causes [observable loss] because [supported or hypothesised mechanism].” If not, it is a topic, not yet an idea.

## Tunnel-vision diagnosis

The recordings show productive self-critique, but also four locks:

1. **Topic lock:** conversation repeatedly returns to sport → awkwardness → stranger approach.
2. **Medium lock:** an app/listing is treated as the default before the rule and causal chain are clear.
3. **Self-as-user lock:** mainly young students’ experience is being used as a proxy for the affected population.
4. **Originality lock:** “is it common?” appears before “is the rule real, costly, and changeable?”

Break the locks by maintaining all territories below as separate hypotheses. Do not combine them or use feature variants as false divergence.

## First-round experience-test prompts

These are research tests, not prototypes to polish. Each should take 15–45 minutes and record consent, observation, and what would falsify the assumption.

For the first round, run **only one** of the sport/permission tests (1–3), **one** appreciation branch test (11–14), and at least two orthogonal tests such as commuter burden, hidden curriculum, or a space-first audit. Do not let the length of either conversation thread dictate the breadth of the evidence.

1. **Court invitation audit:** At two courts, observe only visible invitation signals and ask willing players afterward what would make a solo person welcome or unwelcome.
2. **Arrival-to-belonging diary:** Ask five UNSW solo sport participants to map their first ten minutes: what they knew, feared, read, and did.
3. **“No thanks” script test:** Role-play three ways a group can decline a join request; ask both sides which feels clearest and least humiliating.
4. **Priority-seat co-design interview:** With disability advocates/travellers, map the exact moment of need, request, response, and recovery. Stop if the premise asks for public proof.
5. **Commute capacity map:** Ask commuters to annotate one campus day with travel, fatigue, cost, skipped activities, and moments they would have joined if conditions differed.
6. **Phone-signal intercept:** In a safe campus waiting space, ask volunteers what headphones, a phone, eye contact, and posture mean to them there; compare intended versus interpreted signals.
7. **First-question capture:** Interview first-year students about a question they withheld, why, who could answer, and what the cost was.
8. **Space-first safari:** In 30 minutes each, map rules in a library, transit interchange, sport court, studio, and service counter; capture rule, enforcer, burden, and exception before ideating.
9. **Permission-object sketch:** Make three paper-only mechanisms—a role badge, shared task, and opt-in cue—and ask participants what each authorises and what it makes unsafe.
10. **Backfire check:** For any norm-message draft, ask: “Could showing this make the harmful behaviour feel normal?” Rewrite it as a voluntary, positively approved action or drop it.
11. **Appreciation friction interview:** Ask six people about a sincere appreciation they wanted to send but did not. Separate the feared response, audience, timing, format, relationship history, and whether a reply felt required.
12. **Receiver-first message-format branch test:** Recruit diverse friendship pairs who both opt in. Let receivers choose whether to open, when to open, whether to reply, and whether to keep the content private; compare ordinary text, a crafted/delayed note, and a note explicitly marked “no reply needed.” Measure receiver agency, pressure, obligation, clarity, and desired next action—not generic “impact.” Do not send personal content or disclose relationship details without mutual consent.
13. **Low-contact friendship diary:** Ask people who describe a friendship as low-contact to map what actually maintains it, what silence means, and which expectations they welcome or reject. Look for variation, not confirmation.
14. **Competitor mechanism teardown:** Compare Slowly, Kudoboard, and Letterloop. For each, identify the format overlap, the rule it does *not* address, and the smallest mechanism that would be genuinely different. Vendor pages verify format, not adoption or outcomes.
15. **Gender-policing norm probe:** With diverse friendship pairs and no assumed target gender, ask about a direct appreciation message that felt normal, too intense, jokingly policed, misread, or welcome. Identify the reference group, context, wording, enforcement signal, exception, and whether a ritual would reduce or amplify the pressure. Include a genuine “would not use this” route.
16. **Pitch-demo accessibility rehearsal:** Test a generic room-wide sample letter with an opt-in QR route and an equally prominent spoken/printed/no-device fallback. Require no personal data, scan, disclosure, response, or public reaction; treat it as a demo-flow check, not evidence of emotion or product impact.

## What cannot happen yet

- No selected concept, named target user, or claim of demand.
- No polished sports app, social feed, dashboard, or AI feature set.
- No priority-seat concept without disability-led research and a clear dignity/safety boundary.
- No commute solution that equates inequity with poor personal productivity.
- No pitch claim that phones caused social decline or that a listing will create adoption.
- No claim that instant messaging caused reduced appreciation, that delayed delivery is inherently more meaningful, or that men as a group cannot express care.
- No appreciation product before testing whether the burden is awkwardness, response pressure, group performance, low-contact legitimacy, or something else entirely.
- No claim that a slur or homophobic policing language is acceptable copy, that it represents any group generally, or that it justifies targeting “men” as a monolith. It is raw evidence of one possible enforcement signal to investigate safely.
- No personalised research about judges, personal letter to a judge, compulsory QR scan, required device, public disclosure, or emotional reaction treated as validation. Any pitch interaction must be optional, accessible, and generic.
