# Evidence

## Past brief pattern

Across 2021-2025, SUEDE briefs moved through mental health, remote connection, AI inside a work tool, sustainability and digital literacy. The surface topic changed, but each asked teams to interpret a current human problem rather than merely decorate a feature.

Treat this as pattern recognition, not a prediction of the 2026 topic.

## 2024 judging criteria

- Source: [SUEDE Designathon 2024](https://suede-designathon-2024.devpost.com/)
- Confirmed criteria: Visual Communication, Problem Identification, Solution Approach, Design Innovation and Presentation Skills.
- Observation: no public weighting was provided.
- What this changes for us: this is the strongest available SUEDE-specific model until the 2026 rubric is published.

- Source: [Devpost project page](https://devpost.com/software/hot-topic-jmrv5b)

## 2024: FreTo

- Source: [FreTo case study](https://www.ary4n.com/freto)
- Context: winning response to the sustainability brief.
- Useful observation: the team spent its first hour reading the brief and comparing four problem directions by impact and effort before building.
- Interpretation: choosing the problem was treated as part of execution.
- What this changes for us: time spent selecting an angle is valuable when it reduces the risk of polishing the wrong thing.

The published timeline is more specific: background research began around 12:00, research objectives at 13:00, primary research at 13:20, analysis around 15:30, ideation around 19:15, sketching around 21:00, mid-fidelity work around 22:05, testing around 02:00 and high-fidelity work around 06:00. This is one winning example, not a universal recipe.

## 2025 format and rules

- Sources: [SUEDE 2025 overview](https://suede-designathon-2025.devpost.com/) and [rules](https://suede-designathon-2025.devpost.com/rules)

## What judges tend to reward

- Observation: cited judge priorities include end-user experience, brief fit, idea originality, the demo and storytelling.
- Observation: common negatives include ambiguity, generic or repeated ideas and incomplete requirements.

- Limitation: the 2025 public criteria were listed as TBA, and the 2026 rubric is not yet published. Treat this as a working model and replace it when official criteria arrive.
- New priority: use the explicit 2024 SUEDE criteria before generic Devpost guidance. Generic guidance is supporting evidence only.

## Team discussion: simplicity and purposeful wit in the pitch

- Source: [New Recording 14 raw transcript](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-14.md), recorded 4 September 2026.
- Observation: the speakers repeatedly argued that a technically informed judging audience should still receive a problem explanation simple enough for a non-specialist to understand.
- Observation: Steve Jobs's explanation of the physical keyboard and stylus was discussed as an example of making an unfamiliar problem immediately perceptible.
- Observation: the discussion converged on using humour or wit only when it makes the problem or solution clearer, possibly once or twice, while reading the room and avoiding personal or employer-directed insults.
- Interpretation: the useful device is not comedy by itself. A sharp comparison can compress explanation, demonstrate confidence and make the mechanism easier to remember.
- Limitation: the mono recording has no reliable speaker attribution, and this entry records a working team discussion rather than an approved pitch script.
- What this changes for us: test the problem explanation on a non-specialist; keep any joke only if removing it makes the problem or mechanism harder to understand.

## Initial ideation reality checks: unwritten rules

These checks answer factual questions raised in [Recording 15](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-15.md), [Recording 16](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-16.md), [Recording 17](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-17.md), [Recording 19](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-19.md), and [Recording 20](TRANSCRIPTS/2026-09-04-new-recording-20.md). The recordings repeatedly revisit early sport, social-friction, and later appreciation/friendship threads, so repetition across them is not independent corroboration.

### Pickup-sport coordination is an existing category

- Sources accessed 4 September 2026: [Just Play Australia](https://justplay.com.au/), [OpenSports](https://opensports.net/), [SubbedIn](https://subbedin.com.au/), [Zubs](https://zubs.com.au/) and [TurnUp](https://turnupsport.com/).
- Vendor-stated capability: public product pages describe individual game discovery, published openings, fill-in players, solo registration and coordination. This verifies category overlap, not reliability, uptake or current local supply.
- Official-page statement: [Arc UNSW Social Sport](https://www.arc.unsw.edu.au/sport/on-campus/social-sport) says solo registrants can be placed with others and incomplete teams can be rounded out with individual registrants.
- Interpretation: “list an open sport place and let a solo player claim it” is a useful obvious control, not defensible novelty by itself. The less-solved question is how a person enters an informal group in the live moment without either side carrying an asymmetric risk of intrusion, rejection, skill mismatch or unsafe contact.

### Phones are a possible signal, not a proven root cause

- Sources: [Linares and Sellier's direct replication](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0251451), [Humphreys on phone use and public interaction](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444805058164) and the [ABS 2025 General Social Survey](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/people-and-communities/general-social-survey-summary-results-australia/2025).
- Confirmed observation: the direct replication did not reproduce the earlier claim that the mere presence of a phone reduced strangers' relationship quality. Other work documents phones changing public-interaction practices. The ABS reports weekly face-to-face contact outside the household was lower in 2025 than 2019, but does not attribute that change to phones.
- Unsupported claim: “phones made this generation afraid to approach strangers.”
- Testable interpretation: a phone, headphones, posture or apparent busyness may act as an ambiguous “do not interrupt” signal even when the person is privately open to contact.

### Priority seating contains a stronger hidden rule than “young people should stand”

- Sources: [NSW Passenger Transport Regulation](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/sl-2017-0473), [Transport for NSW hidden-disability support](https://transportnsw.info/news/2024/extra-support-for-hidden-disabilities) and its [Sydney Trains visual story](https://transportnsw.info/document/6460/hiddendisabilities-visualstory.pdf).
- Official policy/guidance: priority or courtesy seating is formally designated for people with accessibility or mobility needs, including hidden disability. Transport for NSW also recognises that someone who needs a seat may be nervous or anxious to ask and encourages passengers to offer.
- Hypothesis requiring affected-person research: appearance-based judgement may create a disclosure burden, but the exact lived rule, enforcement and local consequence have not been verified.
- Safety boundary: do not design public disability ranking, forced disclosure, diagnosis proof or a system that pressures someone to surrender a seat when their own need is invisible.

### Commute burden can shape participation and belonging

- Sources: research on [commute satisfaction, campus participation and perceived success](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trd.2021.102890), [commuting and university participation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2018.06.006) and a recent [Australian design-faculty engagement study](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13384-025-00840-6).
- Confirmed observation: these studies associate commute time, mode or satisfaction with campus attendance, activity participation, flexibility and social opportunities. They do not prove that every UNSW commuter experiences the same consequence.
- Interpretation to test locally: “commuters should absorb travel time and social loss privately, then participate as if they arrived with equal capacity.”
- What this changes for us: investigate group scheduling, late arrival, early departure, rest and missed informal connection before proposing a transport or productivity product.

### Revealing a norm can accidentally reinforce it

- Sources: [Schultz et al.'s field experiment](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01917.x) and [Bicchieri on norm nudging](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/norm-nudging-and-twisting-preferences/1FB6EF21C4947C012BDDAF25300B8A65).
- Confirmed observation: descriptive messages about what others do can create a boomerang effect, while behaviour also depends on what a relevant group is believed to do and approve.
- What this changes for us: do not lead with “nobody talks to strangers anymore” or dramatise harmful behaviour as universal. A concept should identify the relevant group, make a safer desired action possible and show how approval, permission or incentives change.

### Friendship appreciation is a plausible barrier, not a settled problem

- Source: [Kumar and Epley's three gratitude-letter experiments](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618772506), with open data/materials and preregistration linked by the paper.
- Confirmed observation: before delivering gratitude letters, expressers overestimated how awkward recipients would feel and underestimated recipients' reported positive response. Anticipated awkwardness and mood were associated with willingness to express gratitude.
- Boundary: this supports a narrow hypothesis about miscalibrated expectations around gratitude letters. It does not show that direct appreciation is awkward in every friendship, that recipients always welcome it, or that a particular digital format changes the outcome.
- What this changes for us: the test is not “can we make a prettier letter?” It is whether a format can lower anticipated awkwardness and response pressure without scripting or manufacturing intimacy.

### Receiver agency and pitch theatre need separate evidence

- Recordings 19 and 20 shift the team toward a receiver-first framing: a personal care-package/letter anecdote describes one meaningful experience, and a room-wide QR letter is proposed as a pitch moment.
- Boundary: the anecdote is personal evidence only. A scan, applause, visible emotion, or other reaction during a demo cannot establish that a product reduces pressure, changes a norm, or benefits recipients.
- Demo constraint: any interaction must be opt-in, accessible, generic rather than personalised to judges, offer an equally clear non-QR/no-device route, require no personal content or disclosure, and never require a reply. Researching individual judges to personalise a message is excluded as stalkery/privacy-risk.
- What this changes for us: test receiving separately from sending. Measure recipient agency, pressure, obligation, privacy, and desired next action—not a vague “impact” score or performance reaction.

### Reminders can change the sincerity signal

- Source: [Wang, Chaudhry and Koch's 10-study research on gratitude reminders](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000442) (2024; [PMID 38010756](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38010756/)).
- Confirmed observation: reminded thankers were perceived as less genuine and less likeable than spontaneous thankers, including in receiver judgments. Reminders can increase the behaviour while creating a sincerity-signalling burden.
- Boundary: this is not Facebook-specific and does not prove that a reminder-based product is harmful or that any particular design solves the burden. [Frischmann et al.](https://doi.org/10.1177/08944393211008855) is only supporting context that Facebook birthday notifications can drive greetings even on fake assigned birthdays; it does not measure sincerity.
- What this changes for us: do not treat a daily prompt, random assignment, or notification as neutral. Test whether provenance is visible, whether the recipient reads it as obligation, and whether the interaction should be initiated only when the sender has a concrete, self-chosen reason.

### Direct thanks can carry unintended relational meaning

- Source: [Yu and Chaudhry's gratitude-expression research](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000435) (2024).
- Confirmed observation: across its studies, gratitude could signal that relationship expectations had been exceeded; direct thanks was less preferred for close others in some cultural contexts, including a China–US comparison.
- Boundary: this does not establish the recording's “something must be wrong” interpretation, prove an Australian friendship norm, or explain a specific gendered response.
- What this changes for us: in receiver interviews, test whether an unexpected appreciation message is decoded as warmth, distance, debt, a crisis signal, or simply normal. Do not assume the sender and receiver give it the same meaning.

### Constant contact can be both care and pressure

- Source: [Hall and Baym on mobile maintenance expectations](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444811415047).
- Confirmed observation: the study examines how mobile-phone use in close friendship shapes expectations, dependence, overdependence, entrapment, and friendship satisfaction. The relevant tension is mixed rather than simply harmful: maintenance can support friendship and can also become burdensome.
- Unsupported claim: “instant messaging caused people to stop appreciating friends” or “high-contact friendship is inauthentic.”
- What this changes for us: test deliberate craft, chosen delivery, structural closure, and a clear “no reply needed” variant against an ordinary message. Forced delay is only a historical comparison branch, not a current sincerity mechanism. Treat low-contact friendship as one legitimate pattern to investigate, not a preference to impose.

### Masculinity is a context to investigate, not a target stereotype

- Sources: a recent [review of masculinity, social connection, and loneliness](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12121954/) and the Australian Institute of Family Studies' [Ten to Men social connectedness report](https://aifs.gov.au/tentomen/major-report/social-connectedness-among-australian-males).
- Confirmed observation: the review describes how particular masculinity norms can constrain vulnerable self-disclosure and responsiveness in some relationships. Ten to Men found both that many Australian men reported meaningful support and connection and that a meaningful minority reported low support; greater conformity to masculine norms was associated with lower support/relationship satisfaction in its analysis.
- Boundary: neither source permits a claim that all men avoid appreciation, that men are the only affected group, or that gender is the causal mechanism in this team’s context. Friendship norms vary by person, culture, place, relationship, and life stage.
- What this changes for us: recruit across genders and friendship styles. If a gendered pattern appears locally, let participants name the rule and its exceptions; do not begin with “fix men.”

### A possible enforcement signal is not a general rule

- Recording 20 includes the team’s speculation that in some male-friend contexts, direct appreciation may be treated as abnormal, too intense, or misread as sexual; it also includes a quoted homophobic slur as a possible policing signal and immediate uncertainty about whether the pattern is male-specific, generational, or widely shared.
- Boundary: quoted harmful language is preserved in the raw transcript as evidence of a possible enforcement signal. It is never acceptable product or pitch copy, and it cannot support a claim about men, a generation, or any other group.
- Unknowns that block framing: whether there is a shared **injunctive** norm rather than a team habit; its reference group and context; who experiences it; whether a ritual relieves or intensifies gender policing; and why someone expecting rejection would opt in.
- What this changes for us: the existing masculinity sources still support contextual investigation only. Recruit diverse friendship pairs, permit a non-participation answer, and compare whether the recipient and sender experience the same pressure before considering a gender-specific claim.

### Existing appreciation formats are controls, not proof of a solution

- Vendor-stated capability: [Slowly](https://slowly.app/) presents delayed, letter-style digital correspondence; [Kudoboard's delivery guide](https://support.kudoboard.com/hc/en-us/articles/360051516514-How-do-I-schedule-my-Kudoboard-for-delivery-or-deliver-it-ASAP) describes group boards and scheduled delivery; [Letterloop](https://www.letterloop.co/) presents private group newsletters for friends, families, and teams.
- These official pages verify **format overlap** only: delayed letters, group appreciation boards, and private group updates already exist. They do not verify adoption, retention, outcomes, or whether those products reduce sender awkwardness or response pressure.
- What this changes for us: “a digital letter with a delay” and “a collaborative scrapbook board” are existing controls. A credible direction needs to change a specific unwritten rule, such as compulsory reciprocity, performative group contribution, or the legitimacy of low-contact friendship.

### Useful script-changing precedents

- [Human Library](https://humanlibrary.org/) makes difficult questions possible through consent, named roles and a bounded “borrow a person” interaction rather than an instruction to approach strangers.
- [Jokebox](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/jokebox-coordinating-shared-encounters-in-public-spaces/) gives strangers a shared third object and low-stakes common response rather than requiring one person to cold-open a conversation.
- [Design Justice Network principles](https://designjustice.org/read-the-principles) centre the people directly affected, impact over designer intent and existing community knowledge.
- [Invisible theatre](https://beautifultrouble.org/toolbox/tool/invisible-theatre) and the [alienation effect](https://toolbox.beautifultrouble.org/tool/alienation-effect) show how performance can make a familiar power relation strange, but both require careful consent, safety and debrief choices.
- What this changes for us: compare permission structures and shared tasks against spatial, institutional, incentive and non-interaction mechanisms—not merely information about the rule.
